# Picture-Frame Platformer Interview: Questions 341-345

## Question 341: Shared Collectible and Reward Grammar

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: the current app mixes fragments, marks, notes, badges, a
stabilizer, and a final key with generic glow and collectible behavior.
Replacement art needs to preserve each reward's story identity while making
persisted, presented, and already-owned states unmistakable without creating a
second pickup authority.

Question: Which visual grammar should identify route collectibles and rewards?

1. Preserve each reward's unique route-authored silhouette, material, symbol,
   and label, but present it on one shared three-stitch folio tab with a stable
   receipt notch. Before earning, show only the allowed objective or labeled
   empty ledger slot when relevant, never a false world pickup. Persist the
   reward and completion receipt first; then reveal the full artifact, close
   its stitch halo, and fold it into the satchel or ledger as presentation only.
   Revisits show a non-interactive embossed imprint, owned label, and receipt
   status. Mirror every state through texture, description, static,
   reduced-effect, silent, and non-spatial forms without respawning or
   re-awarding it.
2. Let every route invent unrelated reward silhouettes, earned-state cues,
   presentation ordering, ledger marks, revisit behavior, and accessible
   equivalents.
3. Replace every story reward with the same spinning coin, orb, or glowing
   shard, distinguishing routes only by color and particle effect.
4. Let proximity, collision, animation completion, particle visibility, or a
   decorative pickup decide that the reward was earned before a durable domain
   receipt exists, and allow revisits to spawn and grant it again.

## Question 342: Shared Checkpoint Visual Grammar

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: the provisional routes depend on short atomic checkpoints,
exact recovery, and paper-fold transitions, but the current app exposes only
generic objective status and completion feedback. Players need to know where a
safe checkpoint is, whether it has durably saved, and which state will restore
without confusing it with a portal or reward.

Question: Which visual grammar should identify checkpoints?

1. Integrate one non-interactive stitched-bookmark marker into every checkpoint
   landing without changing its safe top edge. Before qualification, its hollow
   tab, checkpoint number, and concise label identify the upcoming save. Only a
   durable domain checkpoint receipt fills the tab, closes its two side
   stitches, and permits the bounded paper-fold acknowledgment. Restoration
   shows the same stable id, filled state, and return arrow before resuming.
   Static, reduced-effect, silent, high-contrast, descriptive, and non-spatial
   forms expose identical saved or unsaved meaning while animation, collision,
   light, and renderer state remain non-authoritative.
2. Let every route invent unrelated checkpoint silhouettes, save indicators,
   numbering, transition effects, restore cues, and accessible equivalents.
3. Mark checkpoints only with route-colored light, particles, or a transient
   checkmark, without a persistent label or visible saved-versus-unsaved state.
4. Treat touching a prop or finishing its animation as proof of saving, even
   when no durable checkpoint receipt exists, and restore to an unlabeled or
   inferred location after interruption.

## Question 343: Decorative Dressing Boundary

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: the mixed-media museum worlds need rich frames, papers, relics,
cases, shadows, and ambient motion, but decoration must never resemble a safe
platform, hazard, interactable, checkpoint, portal, or reward—or silently
acquire collision and input authority.

Question: Which visual grammar should distinguish decorative dressing?

1. Reserve every critical edge, bracket, touch texture, checkpoint bookmark,
   portal aperture, receipt notch, semantic symbol, label, and state-light form
   for gameplay bindings. Dressing uses quieter incomplete contours, lower
   focal contrast, ambient-only motion, and route materials outside declared
   readability, clearance, and interaction masks. It has no semantic collision,
   hit target, valid-path preview, or state mutation; grouped optional
   descriptions identify meaningful scenery. Performance tiers may remove it
   before any critical asset, and missing dressing never blocks play.
2. Let each route decide independently whether props look interactive, collide,
   receive labels, occupy cue space, animate as state feedback, or disappear by
   tier.
3. Give decorative objects the same brackets, glow, edge language, labels,
   pointer response, and motion as gameplay objects so the scene feels
   consistently active.
4. Hide required platforms, hazards, targets, and route clues inside unlabeled
   decoration and rely on trial-and-error touching, camera inspection, or mesh
   collision to discover what matters.

## Question 344: Shared Material Layering Contract

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: safe edges, hazard marks, interaction brackets, checkpoint
bookmarks, and portal states must retain one meaning while the routes move
through map paper, teal gilt, charcoal sketch, crimson placard, olive archive,
cold glass, violet canvas, and gold portal materials.

Question: Which material system should govern gameplay-critical art?

1. Build every critical asset from three bounded layers: one stable semantic
   ink layer for edges, textures, symbols, labels, and state marks; one
   route-authored substrate for paper, wood, gilt, graphite, glass, canvas, or
   relic identity; and one optional patina layer that cannot enter critical
   masks or reduce validated contrast. Author light and dark semantic variants
   plus static and low-tier equivalents, selected only by the pinned route and
   readability profile. Live camera color, room light, effects, and decorative
   materials may influence ambience but never choose meaning.
2. Bake each route's semantic cues directly into unrelated bespoke materials,
   with no shared layer identities, masks, contrast rules, or equivalent
   variants.
3. Use one generic material and distinguish every route, object role, and state
   only through a runtime color tint.
4. Make photoreal camera reflections, sampled room colors, dynamic exposure,
   procedural wear, and environmental lighting the primary source of both
   route identity and gameplay-state readability.

## Question 345: Shared Motion and Focal Hierarchy

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: the current routes already combine breathing loops, moving
targets, jitter, pulses, bounces, unfolds, and portal expansion. Replacement
art adds moving platforms, hazard telegraphs, checkpoint folds, JR poses,
landmark reactions, and ambient museum motion that must remain readable
together and preserve reduced-motion parity.

Question: Which motion hierarchy should govern all eight routes?

1. Keep critical meaning static-first. Deterministic platform and hazard motion
   follows its fixed semantic path and phase; every accepted state-change
   acknowledgment uses a bounded anticipate, act, and settle envelope with one
   primary focal cue per local region. JR and the next required cue outrank
   landmark reaction, while ambient dressing uses slower, smaller motion
   outside critical masks and freezes first under tier or pause. Reduced motion
   replaces travel, scale, shake, and camera movement with stable poses,
   outlines, labels, and short crossfades. Animation may acknowledge accepted
   state but its start, loop, frame, or completion never advances gameplay.
2. Let every route independently choose motion priority, speed, easing,
   simultaneous focal effects, pause behavior, and reduced-motion meaning.
3. Keep every interactive object, landmark, hazard, reward, and decorative prop
   continuously pulsing, floating, jittering, or glowing, using motion intensity
   as the primary way to show importance.
4. Let procedural physics, random animation, camera movement, effect completion,
   or frame timing decide platform paths, hazard phases, accepted interactions,
   checkpoint state, or progression.
