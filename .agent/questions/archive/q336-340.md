# Picture-Frame Platformer Interview: Questions 336-340

## Question 336: Shared Platform Visual Grammar

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: current routes still rely heavily on generic shapes, while an
earlier decision only says critical modules need consistent silhouettes and
edges. Replacement art now needs exact cross-route safe-platform identities
that survive each material family without making renderer meshes authoritative.

Question: Which visual grammar should identify safe platform types?

1. Give every safe landing one continuous high-contrast top lip and double
   ink-or-material edge shared across all routes, while its body uses the local
   map, gilt, sketch, placard, archive, glass, canvas, or portal material.
   Static platforms keep an unbroken edge and quiet texture; moving platforms
   add paired directional notches and a visible path cue; temporary or
   revealable platforms use a torn or dashed secondary edge plus a stable state
   cue. Bind the same non-color meanings to accessible descriptions and keep
   semantic collision independent from every art mesh and LOD.
2. Let every route invent unrelated safe-platform silhouettes, edge treatments,
   motion cues, temporary-state marks, and accessibility descriptions with no
   shared meaning.
3. Keep one identical generic rectangular block for every static, moving,
   temporary, and revealable platform, distinguishing types only by route color
   or animation.
4. Let final decorative meshes and their visible contours generate platform
   collision, landing tops, moving bounds, and route meaning automatically at
   runtime.

## Question 337: Shared Hazard Visual Grammar

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: hazards must remain distinct from safe landing tops across
materials, color perception, motion settings, physical scale, and renderer
tiers while preserving deterministic telegraphs and semantic collision.

Question: Which visual grammar should identify harmful surfaces and hazards?

1. Forbid the safe double top lip on every hazard. Give harmful bounds one
   broken single edge, inward-pointing triangular cuts, and a stable crosshatch
   texture over each route's local material. Use three fixed semantic states:
   quiet dormant marks, a high-contrast notched warning pulse, and a filled
   active boundary. Moving hazards add a visible path cue; shadow hazards keep
   their rune texture. Bind matching accessible labels and optional audio or
   haptic intents, while semantic hazard volumes remain independent from art
   meshes, effects, and LODs.
2. Let each route invent unrelated harmful silhouettes, edge meanings,
   telegraph phases, textures, and accessible descriptions.
3. Mark every hazard only by making it red or brighter than nearby scenery,
   using the same shape and top edge as safe platforms.
4. Hide hazard meaning inside decorative props and derive active collision from
   final meshes or effects without a stable warning phase or semantic bound.

## Question 338: Shared Interactable Visual Grammar

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: glyphs, layers, words, artifacts, reveal symbols, and portal
parts use different route materials, but players need one readable distinction
between available, focused, valid, accepted, invalid, and blocked interaction
states across every input method.

Question: Which visual grammar should identify interactable objects?

1. Give every interactable one stable four-corner bracket silhouette, dotted
   touch texture, route-specific semantic symbol, and concise label. Available
   keeps the brackets open; focus or selection closes an inner bracket and
   thickens the edge; a valid destination shows the matching symbol and one
   semantic preview path; acceptance adds a locked stitch or seal notch.
   Invalid, stale, and blocked results retract the preview, preserve prior
   state, and expose a typed reason. Mirror every state through focus,
   description, reduced-motion, silent, and non-pointer equivalents while the
   domain remains authoritative.
2. Let every route invent unrelated selection marks, valid-target cues,
   acceptance effects, rejection behavior, labels, and accessible equivalents.
3. Mark interactables only with a colored glow or constant pulse, using no
   stable shape, texture, label, focus state, or typed invalid result.
4. Make decorative and gameplay objects visually identical and clickable,
   relying on trial and error to reveal whether an input can mutate route state.

## Question 339: Shared Portal Visual Grammar

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: every route needs a legible destination and transition, but the
current app mixes generic rings, frames, gates, and collectible-like goals.
Replacement art must distinguish locked, eligible, focused, accepted, and
completed portals without letting proximity, camera movement, effects, or
renderer geometry authorize progress.

Question: Which visual grammar should identify route portals?

1. Give every portal one nested frame-and-aperture silhouette over its local
   route material. Locked portals keep a visibly incomplete inner ring with
   labeled unmet motif or fragment sockets; eligibility closes the ring and
   adds one stable entry chevron; focus applies the shared interaction brackets
   outside the aperture with an explicit `Enter` label. Only an accepted domain
   action may start an inward paper-fold transition, and persisted completion
   leaves a stable stitch or seal notch for revisit. Mirror every state through
   non-color texture, description, reduced-motion, silent, and non-spatial
   equivalents while portal volumes remain independent from art meshes and
   effects.
2. Let every route invent unrelated portal silhouettes, lock meanings,
   eligibility cues, entry actions, completion marks, and accessible
   equivalents.
3. Use one glowing ring for every locked, eligible, active, and completed
   portal, distinguishing state only through color, brightness, or pulse speed.
4. Treat the visible opening as the authoritative portal: complete or
   transition when the player, device, camera, or decorative mesh crosses it,
   even without an accepted explicit domain action.

## Question 340: Shared Lighting and Contrast Grammar

Status: provisionally self-answered; user override remains open

Provisional selection: 1

Why it matters: current route recipes reuse base, accent, glow, and danger
colors across many object roles, while Page 07 also treats reveal light and
exposure as part of play. Replacement art must remain readable in changing AR
rooms and performance tiers without making live camera pixels, brightness,
bloom, or effects authoritative.

Question: Which lighting and contrast grammar should govern all eight routes?

1. Use one three-layer semantic lighting contract. A neutral readability field
   preserves JR and every critical edge; one bounded route landmark pool
   establishes local palette and depth; state lights appear only after accepted
   domain events. Safe surfaces, hazards, interactables, and portals retain
   their shape, texture, outline, and label meanings with every effect disabled.
   Clamp exposure, bloom, shadow contrast, and pulse area; never derive state
   from camera pixels or room brightness. High-contrast, static, reduced-effect,
   silent, and low-tier forms preserve the same meaning, dropping decorative
   accents and shadows before any critical cue.
2. Let every route invent unrelated brightness hierarchies, shadow meanings,
   state-light conventions, fallback behavior, and accessibility equivalents.
3. Use route-colored glow intensity as the only cue for safe, harmful,
   interactive, eligible, and completed states, with no stable non-light form.
4. Require real darkness, camera exposure, device movement, gaze, flashlight
   aim, or bloom visibility to reveal or advance semantic state, and allow
   performance tiers to remove critical lights when budgets tighten.
